Picking up stitches lets you add a neckband, button band, border, or collar directly to an existing piece. The method here draws new loops of working yarn through the edge. The important choices are where to insert the needle and how many new stitches the finished edge needs.

Three stitches for every four rows is a possible starting ratio along a stockinette side edge, not a rule for every gauge or border. Follow the pattern's construction and pickup count first.

## Pick up vs pick up and knit

Some instructions distinguish lifting existing edge loops onto a needle from drawing new yarn through the edge, called **pick up and knit**. Many patterns call the latter simply **pick up**. Check what the designer means. Neither operation is the same as returning held live stitches to a needle or repairing a dropped stitch.

For the knitwise method below, assume conventional right-handed knitting:

1. Hold the fabric with its right side facing you and the edge to be worked uppermost. Start where the pattern specifies; on a practice edge, start at the right end and progress left. Rotate the piece as needed along a neckline.
2. Keep the working yarn behind the fabric and leave a tail long enough to weave in. Insert the right needle through the chosen position from front to back without splitting the yarn.
3. Wrap the working yarn as for a knit stitch and draw a new loop through to the front. Keep that loop on the needle and hold the tail while making the next stitch.
4. Move to the next planned pickup position, leaving skipped positions unworked. Keep the loops comfortably on the needle shaft rather than tightening them on its tip.

Each new loop counts as one stitch. With the conventional Western mount, its right leg is in front; other knitting styles can mount stitches differently. Work the leading leg so the stitch opens without crossing, unless the pattern asks for a twisted stitch.

## Along a horizontal edge (cast-on or bind-off)

On a stockinette bind-off edge, insert through the center of the complete V immediately below the bind-off chain, right through the fabric. This aligns the new stitch with its column. Do not confuse that V with the sideways links of the bind-off chain, or merely lift its two legs onto the needle.

At a cast-on edge, turn the piece so that edge is uppermost. Locate the complete Vs in this orientation just inside the cast-on. They are offset by half a stitch from the original upward-running columns. On a flat piece there can be one fewer complete V; use the pattern's endpoint treatment to achieve its total rather than counting a partial end loop twice. Cast-on structures differ, so test the placement on a swatch.

With matching stitch gauges, 1:1 is a useful starting point: 80 bound-off stitches can supply 80 new stitches. A different border gauge or pattern count can require skipped positions. Picking up under selected edge strands is another method, with a different seam or alignment; it is not interchangeable with the through-the-center method described here.

## Along a vertical edge (side of the fabric)

For a stockinette side edge, insert through the fabric in the space between the outermost stitch column and the next column. Work at the chosen row positions. This leaves one stitch column as a seam allowance on the wrong side. It is not the mattress-stitch action of lifting a horizontal bar for sewing.

The relevant ratio is **the new border's stitch gauge divided by the original fabric's row gauge**, measured over the same length and in the intended finished state. For a border of 5 stitches per inch and a body of 7 rows per inch, it is 5/7. Equivalently, 20 stitches and 28 rows per 4 inches give 20/28 = 5/7 ≈ 0.714. These are illustrative gauges, not required ones. [Measure your gauge](/articles/how-to-measure-knitting-gauge/) before choosing a rate.

Over 28 edge rows, pick up at positions 1, 2, 4, 5, and 7 in each group of seven. Skip positions 3 and 6. Four groups use all 28 positions: 20 picked up and 8 skipped. By comparison, 3 out of 4 gives 21 stitches over 28 rows, and 7 out of 10 gives a different approximation. They are not automatically close enough for the same band.

### Picking up along a slipped-stitch selvedge

A slipped-stitch chain often has one link spanning two rows. One pickup under both strands of each link therefore gives only one stitch per two rows. Use it only if it meets the required count and fit; ribbing drawing in does not make an insufficient count correct. If more positions are needed, work into the row spaces one column inside the chain, as above, or follow the pattern's specific selvedge method. Do not force several loops into one loose link without testing the result.

### Picking up along a garter stitch edge

One garter ridge represents two rows. One method is to insert front to back between successive ridges, one stitch column inside the edge, and draw through new yarn. Another is to pass under the outermost downward-curving purl bump of each ridge from bottom to top, without splitting it, then draw yarn through that bump. These are different placements; choose one consistently for that section.

One stitch per ridge means one per two rows and can suit garter fabric. Check the actual edge length and new border gauge before using it; there is no automatic need for an extra stitch every few ridges.

## Different ratios for different borders

Ribbing, garter, and seed stitch need the gauge of that border in its intended state. Ribbing's relaxed width differs from its stretched width, and neither garter nor seed stitch has a universal 3-for-4 pickup rate. An attached i-cord has its own attachment rhythm in the pattern.

If no count is given, test the border on a sample edge with the project yarn. Work enough of the border to judge its width, depth, and stretch. Treat it only as the yarn care instructions allow, let it dry and relax, then assess the fit. Blocking cannot reliably correct a wrong count or a constricting pickup row.

## Along a curved edge (necklines)

Mark horizontal, vertical, and shaped sections separately. Use the horizontal method on bound-off sections and the row-edge method along the sides. On diagonal shaping, follow a smooth path inside the stepped edge rather than entering large holes at the steps. Use the pattern's section counts or the actual diagonal length and border gauge; a diagonal has no fixed rate halfway between horizontal and vertical.

Assign every shared boundary or corner to just one section. If a corner needs an extra pickup to bridge a gap, use firm adjacent fabric, include that stitch in the pickup total, and follow the pattern for any later decrease. Corner shaping is a separate operation: a V-neck may need decreases, whereas an outward-turning border may need increases on later rows. Extra pickup stitches alone do not replace that shaping.

## Where to insert the needle

The one-column seam allowance suits the stockinette side method above. Picking through the edge stitch itself can reduce bulk but changes the join. A slipped, garter, sewn, or shaped edge can need a different placement. Choose sound fabric beyond an existing seam allowance, not a loose sewing strand. Be consistent within comparable sections; changing method at a change of edge structure is legitimate.

## Distributing stitches evenly

Mark sections before starting. For equal lengths of the same edge structure, distribute a specified total as evenly as possible. Mark boundaries **between** pickup positions so no endpoint belongs to two sections.

For example, divide 140 row positions into four sections of 35. To pick up 100 stitches, take 25 per section: five repeats of the 5-from-7 sequence above. Each section uses 35 positions, picking up 25 and skipping 10; overall, 100 picked up + 40 skipped = 140.

If the required total is 102, allocate 26, 25, 25, 26 for symmetry. In each outer section, also use position 17, previously skipped: those sections now skip 9 rather than 10. The result is 102 picked up + 38 skipped = 140. First and last positions are used once, not added again at the markers.

Check the border repeat before choosing a total. In the round, plain k1p1 requires a multiple of 2 and k2p2 a multiple of 4. Worked flat, k2p2 starting and ending with k2 needs 4n + 2 stitches before any separate edge stitches. Thus 102 = 4 × 25 + 2 fits that flat layout; with one extra edge stitch at each end, the total is 104. Those two stitches must be included in the pickup allocation or added as the pattern directs. Recheck length and fit as well as divisibility.

If 104 stitches are to be picked up from those same 140 positions, allocate 26 to every section by using position 17 in all four sections: 104 picked up + 36 skipped = 140.

## Using a crochet hook

For a firm edge, insert the hook at the same chosen position, catch the working yarn behind the fabric, and pull a new loop forward. Transfer it to the knitting needle without twisting. The hook changes the tool, not the count or pickup rate.

## Testing pickup with waste yarn

Smooth waste yarn can help you rehearse placement and count before repeating with project yarn. It does not prove the final fit if its thickness or elasticity differs. Assess fit with the actual yarn and border stitch pattern.

## Common mistakes

**Flaring or puckering.** Too many stitches can make a border flare; too few can pull the original edge in. Also check tension, insertion depth, border gauge, shaping, and bind-off elasticity. Redo the pickup when its count or distribution is wrong. Changing needle size or adding a later decrease row does not remove excess fabric already trapped at the join.

**Uneven spacing.** Check each marked section and spread skipped positions. Equal counts suit equal sections only when their length and structure justify them.

**Gaps at corners.** Avoid loose edge loops and double-counted corners. A small remaining gap may be closed with a yarn tail without pulling the edge tight; see the [seaming guide](/articles/seam-knitted-pieces/). Do not automatically add three corner stitches.

**Twisted stitches or a visible ridge.** Check mounting, the working side, and the actual insertion route. Moving deeper is not a universal fix and can add bulk. Weave in the ends without restricting stretch and finish according to the yarn's care instructions.

## Picking up for steeks

A steek is a planned opening cut into knitted fabric. Use a tested method suitable for the yarn: crochet reinforcement is not sufficient for every smooth or non-felting yarn. Complete any required reinforcement before cutting. A picked-up band is not a substitute.

Pick up on the garment side of the steek allowance at the line specified by the method, not immediately beside the cut or from cut yarn ends. The allowance must still be secured or enclosed. Depending on the construction, the band can be picked up before or after cutting; follow that sequence.

## Common questions

**What size needle for picking up?**
Use the pattern's border needle and verify the border gauge. A smaller needle or hook may help you enter a tight edge; transfer to the working size without making the pickup row constrict. Smaller is not automatically neater.

**Right side facing for pickup?**
For the knitwise method here, yes. When working flat, turn after pickup and follow the wrong-side row instructions. When continuing in the round, do not turn unless told to: the right side remains facing. A wrong-side row does not necessarily mean purling every stitch.

**How many stitches for a button band?**
Follow the pattern. Without a count, multiply the intended band length by the band's stitches per unit of length, then check repeats, endpoints, and intended stretch. Use the original edge's row count to distribute that total.

**Why does my pickup row look uneven from the right side?**
Check whether insertion depth, spacing, or loop tension varies. Correct the cause before re-picking; the appropriate placement can change at a corner or different edge structure.

**Can I add length along a finished bound-off edge?**
Yes, but the retained bind-off makes a ridge and may limit stretch. Continuing from the bind-off normally continues the original knitting direction. Working outward from a cast-on reverses direction and can shift the columns by half a stitch. Neither promises an invisible join; test the fabric and pattern first.

**My pickup count matches the pattern but the edge ripples. Why?**
Your actual edge length, border gauge, or pickup tension may differ. Check those before changing the count. Planned increases or decreases can reconcile the pickup total with a later pattern repeat, but they cannot guarantee a flat join with an unsuitable pickup rate.
