Knitting patterns have their own language and formatting conventions. Most published patterns are built from familiar sections: materials and gauge near the top, then row-by-row instructions using common abbreviations, with sizing presented as parenthetical numbers throughout. The first time you open one, it looks like a wall of shorthand. But once you recognize the sections, the whole thing becomes readable.

## The header: what you need before you start

Most patterns start with a block listing what to gather before casting on.

The yarn listing names a specific yarn (or a generic weight) and tells you how much to buy, in yardage, skein count, or both. If you're substituting, use the yarn weight as a starting point, then check gauge, fiber behavior, and total yardage. The [yarn substitution guide](/articles/how-to-substitute-yarn/) covers this in detail.

Needles: the recommended size and sometimes the type (straight, circular, DPNs). Sweaters with ribbed edges often list two sizes here, smaller for the ribbing, larger for the body.

Gauge specifies stitches and rows or rounds over a stated distance, often 4 inches or 10 cm, in a stated stitch pattern. Use the pattern's actual units and blocked or unblocked measurement conditions. The suggested needles are a starting point; choose the size that gives the required gauge. [Swatch before starting](/articles/how-to-measure-knitting-gauge/).

Notions are additional supplies: stitch markers, cable needles, yarn needles, buttons, zippers. Not every pattern needs them, but when listed, you want them on hand before you cast on.

Finished measurements give the dimensions of the completed project by size. Skill level ratings (beginner, intermediate, advanced) are subjective and vary by designer, but they give a rough sense of what techniques you'll encounter.

## What "skill level" actually means

Skill labels depend on the publisher. The [Craft Yarn Council project levels](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/project-levels) are one published system, not a rule every designer follows. Read the publisher's definition and the actual techniques required. A short-row section or a cable alone does not determine the difficulty of the whole project.

Before choosing a pattern, check which techniques you already know and which you want to practise: knit and purl stitches, shaping, working in the round, charts, cables, lace or finishing. Read the relevant instructions before deciding whether the project suits you.

## The notes section

Often skipped, frequently important. Designer notes may include critical information that doesn't fit elsewhere: which gauge to measure (blocked or unblocked), whether to slip the first stitch of every row for clean selvedges, how the construction works (top-down, bottom-up, one-piece yoke), how the schematic measurements were taken, and any quirks of the pattern.

Read the notes before starting. If they say "all measurements after blocking," measure after that treatment so you can compare your work with the stated measurements.

Read the finishing instructions too: seaming, bands, weaving in ends and the specified finishing treatment belong to the project. Check that the treatment is suitable for your yarn.

## Sizes and selecting yours

Follow the size order stated in your pattern, not an assumed universal order. For this illustrative sequence, the sizes are XS (S, M, L, XL): cast on 72 (80, 88, 96, 104) stitches. XS uses 72, S 80, M 88, L 96 and XL 104. Choose one number, not their sum.

Before starting, figure out which size you're making and mark it throughout the pattern. Some knitters highlight with a marker. Others photocopy and circle the relevant numbers. A few write out only their size's numbers on a separate sheet.

Check whether the sizing table gives body measurements or finished garment measurements. Compare the finished chest circumference with your body circumference and the intended ease; do not add ease again to a body-size range already used to select the intended fit. A schematic may give a flat width rather than a full circumference: read its labels. S/M/L labels have no universal measurements. The [sizes and fit guide](/articles/knitting-pattern-sizes-and-fit/) goes deeper.

## Abbreviations vs special stitches

Start with the pattern's own abbreviation key and special-stitch definitions. The English abbreviations k, p, k2tog, ssk and yo are common, but neither abbreviations nor techniques are universal across publishers and languages.

Read the key before starting. Not to memorize it, just scan for anything unfamiliar. If you spot something you don't recognize, look it up or practice the technique before you encounter it mid-row.

Some patterns also include a separate "special stitches" or "stitch glossary" section that defines techniques the abbreviations don't fully capture. A pattern might use "MB" (make bobble) in the instructions, with the actual bobble construction spelled out in the stitch glossary. Treat that glossary as part of the pattern, not an appendix. Skipping it means hitting a row that says "MB across" and not knowing what to do.

The [knitting abbreviations glossary](/tools/knitting-abbreviations/) is a searchable reference covering standard abbreviations.

## Reading the instructions

Pattern instructions are written row by row or section by section. A few formatting conventions worth recognizing.

Row and round numbers tell you where you are. "Row 1 (RS):" means first row, right side facing. "Rnd 3:" is the third round in circular knitting.

Stitch instructions read left to right: "K5, p3, k2tog, yo, k to end" means knit 5, purl 3, knit two together, yarn over, then knit every remaining stitch. Commas separate actions within a row, each one the next thing to do. "K to end" and "p to end" mean keep going until you run out of stitches.

For that last instruction, start with 20 stitches: knit 5 (15 left), purl 3 (12 left), knit two together (10 left, one new stitch), yarn over (still 10 left, one new stitch), then knit those 10. Final count: 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 10 = 20. The yarn over balances the decrease; it does not use the next stitch.

## Repeats

The examples below use the repeat conventions described by the [Craft Yarn Council](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/how-to-read-knitting-pattern). The stitch counts here are practice examples with no extra edge stitches.

**Asterisk repeats:** `*K2, p2; rep from * to end.` With 20 stitches, each unit uses and produces four stitches. Work five units in total, leaving no unworked stitches and 20 stitches after the row. This instruction requires a multiple of four.

**Bracket repeats:** `[K2, p2] 5 times.` Work the group five times in total: 5 × 4 = 20 stitches. If you work the group once and then repeat it five **more** times, that is six executions and 24 stitches. Read the exact wording.

**Parenthetical repeats:** `(K1, p1) across.` With 20 stitches, work ten two-stitch groups; the final count is 20. Here the parentheses group a repeat. Elsewhere they can contain sizes or operations to work into the same stitch: follow the surrounding instruction.

Some patterns mix styles. The key is recognizing where the repeat starts, where it ends, and how many times. The [pattern repeats article](/articles/knitting-pattern-repeats/) gets into complex and nested repeats.

## "At the same time"

"At the same time" means following two instructions during the same section. For example, continue the stitch pattern while decreasing one stitch at each end of every fourth row.

For a complete practice schedule, start this section with 80 stitches, call its next row row 1, and decrease on rows 4, 8 and 12 only. Each decrease row removes two stitches: 80 → 78 → 76 → 74. Work all intervening rows in pattern without shaping. The section contains 12 rows, not just the three decrease rows. In a real pattern, check where the count starts, the first decrease row and how many times to decrease; "every fourth row" alone does not supply those details.

One of the more confusing instructions in knitting patterns, and it gets [its own article](/articles/at-the-same-time-knitting/).

## Reverse shaping

An instruction such as "Work as for Right Front, reversing all shaping" asks you to mirror the shaping. Left and right name the wearer's sides. Neck and armhole shaping move to the opposite edges; buttonholes follow the separate button-band instructions and must not automatically be duplicated.

Write out the second side before knitting. A decrease at the start of a right-side row can move to the end of a right-side row; it does not automatically become a wrong-side decrease. Check the required decrease direction. Standard bind-offs at the start of a row need separate planning for the opposite edge, so check the pattern's row and side instructions instead of moving every operation mechanically.

## Written vs charted instructions

A chart is a grid of stitch instructions. One symbol can use several stitches or create new ones, so counting boxes is not always the same as counting stitches.

A common convention, explained in [Tin Can Knits' chart guide](https://blog.tincanknits.com/2014/06/06/how-to-read-a-knitting-chart/), reads from bottom to top: right-side rows from right to left, wrong-side rows from left to right; rounds from right to left when the right side faces you throughout. Check the row numbers and notes. If some rows are omitted, work them as the text directs; do not skip knitting them or assume they are all purl.

Some knitters find charts faster because you can see the pattern visually. Others prefer written instructions. Many patterns offer both. If both are provided and they disagree, check the designer notes and errata before assuming either version is correct.

### Common chart symbols

Symbols belong to a chart's own legend. In the [Craft Yarn Council chart key](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/knit-chart-symbols), for example, the knit/purl action changes with the side being worked. A filled square can indicate a colour in another chart, so its appearance alone does not mean "no stitch".

- Knit/purl symbols: check both the RS and WS meanings.
- Yarn over: creates one stitch without consuming an existing stitch.
- `k2tog`: uses two stitches and leaves one; `p2tog` is the WS action for the corresponding CYC symbol.
- `ssk`: uses two stitches and leaves one; the corresponding CYC WS action is `ssp`.
- A centered double decrease needs its own definition. In [Purl Soho's s2kp](https://www.purlsoho.com/create/slip-slip-knit-pass-s2kp/), slip two stitches together as if to knit two together, knit one, then pass both slipped stitches over it: three become one. Slipping one, knitting two together and passing the slipped stitch over is a different decrease.
- Cable symbols can span several stitches.
- A cell explicitly labelled "no stitch" uses and creates no stitches. Move to the next chart cell without slipping or working a stitch on the needle.

These are examples of named conventions, not a replacement for your pattern's key. A chart's repeat box marks only the repeated section: work any stitches outside it as instructed.

## Stitch counts and checking your work

A checkpoint might say "60 (68, 76, 84, 92) sts". In the XS (S, M, L, XL) order used above, these are XS 60, S 68, M 76, L 84 and XL 92. Each is 12 fewer than its corresponding cast-on count. This is a separate illustrative checkpoint, not the result of the three decrease rows above.

Count at each checkpoint. If the count differs, check the selected size, repeat totals and shaping, then the pattern's corrections before deciding where the error lies. Catching a discrepancy early limits how much you may need to undo.

If the pattern doesn't include stitch counts, calculate your own. Cast on 80, decrease 8, you should have 72. Write the numbers in the margin.

## Pattern errata

Check the designer's or publisher's pattern page for corrections (errata) and the version you are using before starting. Reader project notes can help identify a question, but distinguish them from an official correction.

If an instruction remains unclear after checking the key, notes and errata, ask the designer or publisher. Do not assume either that you made a mistake or that the pattern is wrong.

## Working from PDF vs paper

For a PDF pattern, choose a format in which you can read the instructions and mark your progress comfortably.

Printing is the simplest option. A paper copy you can mark up, fold, and ignore battery life on. The downside is wasted paper for one-time use and the difficulty of carrying multi-page sweater patterns.

Reading on a tablet works for many knitters. A large tablet in a stand is the common setup. PDF annotation apps let you highlight your size, mark progress, and add notes without printing. Check the screen timeout before you start a dense row.

Phone reading is awkward for full patterns but fine for brief lookups. Tiny text and frequent scrolling fight against following dense instructions.

A printable summary helps either way. Take a phone photo of the key row counts and size column, print it small, and clip it to your project bag. Means you don't have to find the right page mid-row.

## FAQ

**Should I read the whole pattern before starting?**
At least skim it. You don't need to understand every instruction yet, but a read-through flags techniques to learn, supplies to buy, and structural decisions you should know about upfront.

**What does "work even" mean?**
Continue the established stitch pattern without shaping; the stitch count stays constant. This does not mean working only even-numbered rows. In lace, keep any increases and decreases that balance within the established stitch pattern.

**What does "as established" or "as set" mean?**
Continue the established pattern and its row sequence, keeping motifs aligned. For 2 × 2 ribbing, knit the knits and purl the purls as they face you. If shaping is also specified, follow it while maintaining the remaining pattern; do not restart the repeat blindly at a changed edge.

**What does "ending with a WS row" mean?**
Finish a wrong-side row, then turn the work so the right side faces you for the next row. WS names the side of the fabric, not a requirement to purl every stitch.

**Is there a standard format all patterns follow?**
There is no format used by every publisher. Locate the materials, gauge, size information, instructions, key and notes yourself; do not assume every pattern supplies every detail.

**What if the abbreviation key is missing or incomplete?**
Consult the publisher's glossary first. A general list such as the Craft Yarn Council's helps with common English terms, but cannot resolve an undefined special stitch. Ask the designer if the intended action is still unclear, including how to slip a stitch or where to hold the yarn.

**Can I trust free patterns as much as paid ones?**
Price alone does not establish accuracy. Check for clear instructions, size and gauge information, support and published corrections in either free or paid patterns. Reader project notes are supplementary information, not an official correction.
