A row counter can be a mechanical dial, a standalone electronic device, or an app on a phone or tablet. “Physical” does not mean battery-free, and “digital” does not necessarily mean an app. Choose by the information you need to record and how you prefer to enter it.

The counters discussed here depend on your input. Turning a dial, pressing a button, speaking a command or advancing linked counters does not detect a completed knitted row. Count a full row when knitting flat or a full round when knitting in the round.

## Mechanical row counters

Mechanical counters use number wheels or a push-button mechanism without batteries. Needle-mounted, pendant and tabletop designs offer different ways to keep the counter within reach. Check the mounting and size range of the particular model; a barrel shape alone does not establish compatibility with circular needles.

**What they offer.** A mechanical counter can suit screen-free knitting. Some knitters prefer the feel of a click or dial to a touchscreen. Try the number size, operating force and position with your own hands and usual lighting; neither category is automatically easier to use.

**What to check.** A simple single-count counter holds one current number, not a project history. Check how to adjust it downward, reset it, lock it and handle the display limit. These are different operations. For example, [Clover Mini Knitting Counter, model 3118](https://clover-usa.com/products/knitting-counter-mini-kacha-kacha), advances by one through 0–99, has a lock and uses the number wheels for resetting. Its instructions do not establish every counter's behavior or what happens after the highest displayed value. Record any hundreds separately if you use a two-digit counter for a longer piece.

A lock can help protect the setting, but check the count after carrying the work. If a number seems wrong, compare it with your notes and the knitted fabric rather than assuming it cannot be checked.

## Standalone electronic counters

An electronic finger counter is both physical and digital. It has a display and needs power, but it need not involve a phone. [Prym's electronic row counter 611883](https://www.prym.com/de/automatischer-reihenzaehler) has a replaceable battery, button-operated counting and reset, and a memory function for the last value, according to the manufacturer. Despite the German product name “Automatischer Reihenzähler”, its description specifies a button press.

That memory is not evidence of separate project records, undo or a complete history. Check the exact model's decrement function, maximum count, lock and behavior during a battery change. The product page does not settle all of those questions. A finger strap keeps the device close; you still operate its button. Do not assume every small counter uses an LED display.

## Row counter apps

An app may collect counts and records for [multiple projects](/articles/organize-knitting-projects/). Look for the functions you actually need: changing a count, undoing a supported change, tracking repeats, reminders or session records. A saved number, an undo history and a record of working time are separate things.

For a documented example, [My Row Counter's counter guide](https://rowcounterapp.com/counter.html) describes reminders at selected values, repeat resets and secondary counters linked to the main counter by default. They are not necessarily independent. Configure the starting value and links to match your counting convention; these features do not remove the need to check the pattern.

Saving locally is different from backup, restoration or synchronization between devices. Check account and connection requirements for each function, how to make a recoverable copy and what happens when changing phones. A discharged phone makes its app unavailable until it has power; it does not by itself prove either that the last change was saved or that data was lost.

## Keep the meaning of the number clear

One useful convention is **completed rows**, starting at 0 and increasing by one only after finishing a row. At 47, row 47 is complete and row 48 is next. If a decrease belongs in row 52, finish rows 48–51 first: four rows. A reminder that appears only when this counter reaches 52 would arrive after that row, so configure it for the point before the action and check the app's behavior.

If you rip back three complete rows from a verified 47, the new completed count is 44 and row 45 is next. Adjust the count using the model's supported controls or record the corrected value separately. Resetting to zero is not decrementing by three, and undoing a tap does not undo knitting.

For an eight-row repeat starting at row 1, 24 completed rows means three full repeats and repeat row 1 next. Keep the total at 24; record repeat position separately. For simultaneous shaping or paired sleeves, label each counter or note with its piece, purpose and starting point. Paper columns and separate counters can do this too.

## Choose for your knitting habits

For a single count or a screen-free session, a mechanical counter may be enough. For a compact display without a phone, consider an electronic counter. If keeping several counts and notes together matters, an app may suit you. None is universally best for beginners, shaping or lace, and none guarantees accurate input.

Compare readability, grip, accidental presses, carrying arrangements and possible distractions. Hardware has a purchase cost; for apps, distinguish a free download, ongoing free features, a trial, a paid unlock and a subscription. Check the relevant store, platform and currency before comparing costs.

You can use a clicker for a travel sock and an app for a sweater. When switching methods, copy the verified count and its meaning. A spare counter is only a current backup if you update it. If one click represents five cast-on stitches, four clicks mean 20 stitches, not four rows; label that unit separately.

## FAQ

**Do row counter apps work offline?**
Check the specific functions you need. Local storage alone does not establish offline access to login, imported patterns or every feature. Try the intended workflow before relying on it away from a connection, and check backup separately.

**Can I use my smartwatch as a row counter?**
[My Row Counter documents Apple Watch and Wear OS support](https://rowcounterapp.com/features.html). Check your watch, phone and current store requirements, including how counts transfer. Tapping a watch is manual input; whether it interrupts you less is a personal preference.

**Are there row counters for circular needles?**
Yes. A tabletop counter does not need to fit the needle, and Clover 3118 can be worn on a cord. For a counter attached to a needle or project, check its fitting and whether it interferes with stitches or yarn. There is no single best attachment for everyone.

**What about electronic finger counters?**
Check the model rather than assuming “no reverse” or “no memory”. Prym 611883 documents memory of the last value; this is different from separate histories for multiple projects. Its button still needs a deliberate press.
