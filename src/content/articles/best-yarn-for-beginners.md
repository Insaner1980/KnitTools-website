The yarn aisle is overwhelming when you don't know what you're looking at. Walls of color, different thicknesses, labels full of unfamiliar terms. A practical choice for learning basic stitches is worsted weight (CYC category 4) in a light, solid color, a smooth acrylic or wool/acrylic blend that suits your budget and feels good to you. Bamboo or wood needles around US 7–9 are a starting point; check the yarn label. For a first project, the choice is simpler than it looks. You want something forgiving, comfortable to handle, and inexpensive enough that mistakes don't feel expensive.

## Weight: start with worsted

Worsted weight (within CYC category 4, Medium) is a good starting point for practicing basic stitches. Here, weight means thickness, not the weight of the ball in grams.

The stitches are large enough to see clearly. You can watch yarn wrap around the needle, identify individual stitches, and spot mistakes before they become problems. In thinner yarn, stitches blur together and errors hide until you've already passed them three rows back.

With a medium yarn, you need fewer stitches to cover the same area than with a fine yarn. That makes a small practice piece a manageable first goal.

US 7–9 (4.5–5.5 mm) is the CYC guideline for this category, not a rule for every yarn. Start with the yarn label and adjust to the fabric and gauge your pattern needs.

Many beginner patterns use worsted weight or another medium yarn, so the pattern options are wide open.

DK weight (CYC category 3, Light) also works. Its guideline is US 5–7 (3.75–4.5 mm). If you are choosing between worsted and DK, follow your pattern; thickness alone does not determine drape.

For initial practice, I would leave lace, fingering, and sport until later because the smaller stitches take more attention to see. Bulky and super bulky are also options if you like larger needles. Pick a combination you can hold comfortably; thick yarn does not prevent you from learning good technique.

## Fiber: acrylic or wool blend

**Acrylic** is a practical option when you want to keep the cost of practice down. Choose a smooth yarn whose stitches are easy to see, and check the actual price and care instructions on the label.

Try knitting and undoing a few rows before committing to a project. How well a yarn survives repeated ripping back depends on its construction and condition, not just its fiber. Washing and blocking are not a guarantee that uneven tension will disappear.

**Wool/acrylic blends** are another useful starting point if you like their feel. Softness, durability, drape, and care vary with the blend, yarn construction, and knitted fabric. Adding acrylic does not by itself make a wool blend machine washable.

**Pure wool** is also suitable for a first project. Its natural elasticity and recovery can make it pleasant to work with. Untreated wool can felt with moisture and agitation. Follow the manufacturer’s washing and drying instructions, including for superwash wool; the name is not permission to use any wash cycle.

**Cotton** has less elastic recovery than wool, although cotton knitted fabric can still stretch. It is a sensible first choice for a dishcloth if that is what you want to make. For a practice piece, choose a yarn that feels comfortable to handle; blends and construction can change how cotton behaves.

## Color: light and solid

Light colors show stitches more clearly than dark. Cream, light grey, pale blue, light pink, soft yellow. You need to see stitch structure while learning, and dark yarn makes this harder.

Solid colors show stitch definition better than variegated. Multi-color yarn is visually busy and makes it hard to see individual stitches, spot mistakes, and read the fabric. Save it for after you can knit without examining every stitch.

Tonal yarns (subtle color variation within one shade) are a reasonable middle ground if you find pure solids visually boring. They show stitches well enough to learn with.

Temporary constraint. Once you're comfortable with the basics, knit in whatever color you want.

## How much to buy

For a first project (scarf or dishcloth):

Choose the pattern first and check its required yarn length. A 100 g ball does not have a fixed yardage, even within one weight category. Divide the required length by the length per ball and round up, allowing for swatching if the pattern does not include it. For example, a requirement of 360 m with 150 m per ball gives 360 ÷ 150 = 2.4, so buy 3 balls. This is a calculation example, not a scarf estimate.

If you need several balls, choose the same dye lot. Dye lots matter even for solid colors. Two skeins of "natural" cream from different lots can have a visible difference, and the change between them may show as a line across the work. Check the dye lot number on the label and grab from the same one if you're buying multiple skeins.

For a few practice swatches, start with one ball rather than buying a large supply. For a finished scarf or dishcloth, use the pattern's quantity instead of assuming one or two balls will be enough.

## Reading the label

Yarn labels carry the information you need to pick the right yarn. Worth getting comfortable with what's on them.

If the label uses a CYC numbered yarn symbol, 4 means Medium, a category including Worsted, Afghan, and Aran. These names are not exact substitutes for checking gauge. Not every manufacturer uses CYC symbols. The recommended needle size is also on the label, given in US, metric, or both. Gauge is usually expressed as stitches per 4 inches over stockinette. Yardage and meterage tell you how much yarn is in the skein. Fiber content lists what the yarn is made of (100% acrylic, 80% acrylic 20% wool, etc.). Care instructions tell you whether it's machine washable.

For a first practice piece, look for a smooth medium yarn with a needle recommendation around US 7–9 (4.5–5.5 mm). If machine washing matters to you, choose a yarn whose care instructions explicitly allow it.

## What to avoid

**Novelty or very fuzzy yarn** (eyelash, ladder, bouclé, faux fur). Its texture can obscure the stitch loops, making mistakes harder to see. For learning to read and undo stitches, choose a smooth yarn first.

**Very dark colors.** Black yarn on dark needles in dim lighting is an exercise in frustration. Dropped stitches and miscrossed stitches are harder to catch.

**Yarn that feels too slippery on your needles.** Try a few stitches if a shop sample is available. If keeping the loops on the needle is difficult, choose a different yarn or needle surface. Fiber names such as silk, bamboo, or cotton do not describe the whole yarn construction.

**Splitty yarn.** If the needle repeatedly catches only part of the strand, forming stitches becomes harder. Prefer a smooth yarn that holds together while you knit. Ply count alone tells you neither thickness nor twist firmness; try a sample rather than untwisting shop yarn to predict splitting.

**Hanks instead of balls or cakes.** Hanks are loops of yarn that need to be wound into a ball before you can knit from them. Ask whether the shop offers winding. Trying to knit straight from an unwound hank can leave you with tangled yarn. Stick to skeins, balls, or cakes for your first purchase. If you want a hank, ask the shop to wind it.

## After the first project

Once you've finished a project or two (a [scarf](/articles/knit-first-scarf/) is the classic start) and have basic tension control, your choices open up. You'll start developing preferences for fiber feel, needle materials, specific brands. Probably want to try merino. Maybe sock yarn eventually. Something hand-dyed.

## FAQ

**Is expensive yarn better for learning?**
Not necessarily. Choose a yarn you can afford to practice with and enjoy handling. Price alone does not tell you how easy it is to knit or whether the finished item will suit you.

**Can I learn with yarn I already have?**
Probably. If it's worsted or DK, not too dark, not novelty, not extremely slippery, it'll work. Check the label. If there's no label, the [mystery yarn guide](/articles/identify-mystery-yarn/) can help figure out what you have.

**Does the brand matter?**
For a first project, judge the actual yarn by its thickness, visible stitches, feel, and care instructions. A brand or an acrylic label alone does not guarantee a good match.

**How do I store yarn?**
Clean, dry, away from direct sunlight, which can fade colors. A closed bin in a closet is fine. Moth protection matters for wool and other animal fibers, but it's much less relevant for acrylic.

**Craft store vs local yarn shop for the first purchase?**
Either can work. Compare yarns within your budget. If you want help matching yarn to a pattern or winding a hank, ask what assistance the shop offers.

**Should I touch the yarn before buying?**
Yes, if possible. Squeeze it, rub a strand against your wrist or neck. Some yarns that feel fine on a shelf are scratchy against skin. This matters more for scarves and anything that touches the neck than for a dishcloth, but it's a habit worth building early.
