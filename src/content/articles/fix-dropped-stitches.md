A dropped stitch doesn't necessarily mean ripping back the whole piece. In plain stockinette, a crochet hook can rebuild a single column from its loose loop and the horizontal strands above it. Each strand represents one unravelled row. Garter stitch and ribbing need the knit/purl sequence described below.

These repairs assume an ordinary stitch within the fabric, with no shaping or pattern change in the damaged column. Here, front means the side facing you; back means away from you. That is separate from the garment's right and wrong sides. You are recovering an existing stitch, not picking up new stitches along an edge.

## First move: stop the run

The moment you notice a dropped stitch, slip a locking stitch marker through the live loop: the open loop at the bottom of the ladder. Slide the surrounding stitches away from the needle tips or fit needle stoppers. Secure first; fetch the crochet hook second.

Locate the column and work to its position before repairing it. Keep the loop secured while turning the fabric or changing tools. Do not stretch the ladder apart.

## Fixing a dropped knit stitch

Keep the knit face of stockinette toward you, with the V-shaped stitches visible. The [DROPS step-by-step lesson](https://www.garnstudio.com/lesson.php?id=10&cid=17) shows this repair.

1. Put the hook through the live loop from front to back without crossing its legs. Transfer the loop from the marker to the hook.
2. Find the lowest unused horizontal strand belonging to this column, immediately above the loop. Trace it to the neighboring stitches if the strands cross. It must lie behind the loop.
3. Catch that strand and draw it forward through the loop. The old loop comes off the hook; the new loop stays on it. This restores one row.
4. Repeat with the next strand above, from bottom to top. Use each strand once; do not skip one or pull two through together.
5. Once all the missing rows are restored, turn back to the position for continuing your knitting. Return the loop to its place on the needle. If it is the next unworked stitch, it belongs on the left needle. If it was already worked on this row, it belongs on the right: do not knit that row twice.

For the Western mount used here, an unworked stitch on the left needle has its right leg in front, ready to be worked through the front loop without crossing the legs. Check the mount after turning the work, not only while the loop is on the hook. Other mounting conventions are not inherently wrong; the entry leg must match the mount.

Count the stitches, check the column from both faces, and gently redistribute slack through neighboring stitches before continuing. In a section with 20 stitches and no increases or decreases, one dropped column leaves 19 on the needles plus one loose loop. Rebuilding four ladder strands restores four rows, but returns only one live stitch: 20 in total, not 23.

## Fixing a dropped purl stitch

Keep the face with the purl bumps toward you. This can be the wrong side of stockinette, the right side of reverse stockinette, or a purl column in ordinary ribbing. The [Nimble Needles purl repair](https://nimble-needles.com/tutorials/how-to-fix-a-dropped-stitch-in-knitting/) uses the following direction.

1. Hold the live loop securely and bring the lowest unused ladder strand in front of it, toward you. Move the loop relative to the strand without twisting it.
2. Insert the hook through the loop from back to front. Catch the strand in front and draw it backward through the loop, away from you. Keep the new loop; one purl stitch is restored.
3. For the next level, secure the new loop while withdrawing and reinserting the hook from back to front. Put the next strand in front and repeat. Work upward, one strand per row.

Alternatively, turn the fabric over so the reverse of that purl column faces you and looks like knit Vs. Repair it with the knit method, then turn back. The garment's right and wrong sides have not changed names. Finish with the mounting, count, alignment and tension checks above.

## Fixing a dropped stitch in garter stitch

Flat garter stitch is knitted on every row with the work turned between rows. Viewed from one fixed face, however, its rows alternate between knit and purl. One ridge spans two rows; count individual ladder strands, not ridges.

Keep the same face toward you throughout this repair. Compare the intact stitches beside the lowest missing row to decide whether to rebuild a knit V or a purl bump first; do not always start with knit.

1. For a knit level, put that row's strand behind the loop, insert the hook front to back, and draw the strand forward through the loop.
2. For a purl level, secure the loop while repositioning the hook. Put that row's strand in front, insert the hook back to front, and draw the strand backward through the loop.
3. Alternate these actions upward, using each next strand exactly once. Hold the new loop whenever you reposition the hook. Check that the ridges line up with the fabric beside the repair.

The [DROPS garter-stitch tutorial](https://www.garnstudio.com/video.php?id=505&lang=us) shows the change of hook direction. Four missing rows require four strands and two knit/purl pairs, starting with whichever stitch the neighboring fabric shows. Finish with the same needle-mount, stitch-count and tension checks as above.

## Fixing a dropped stitch in ribbing

Ordinary 1×1 or 2×2 ribbing consists of stable knit and purl columns. Keep one face toward you and trace the damaged column from the intact fabric below it and the pattern; the stitch immediately beside it may belong to a different column type.

For a knit column, repeat the knit repair at every level. For a purl column, repeat the purl repair, or turn to its knit face for the whole repair and then turn back. Do not alternate within a column as in garter stitch. Check the rib sequence and count before continuing. This does not cover brioche, twisted rib or a transition between ribbing and another pattern.

## Catching it before it runs

If a stitch has slipped off but has not unravelled, there is a live loop and no ladder to rebuild. Secure it and replace it in its original position, on the left needle if unworked or the right if already worked on this row. Check its mount and count before continuing; do not make an extra stitch with the working yarn.

A hole alone does not prove a stitch has dropped. An accidental yarn over adds a stitch; a wrong knit/purl stitch is still present; a deliberately slipped stitch stays on a needle. Intentional dropped-stitch patterns have their own instructions. Look for the loose live loop and compare the count with the pattern.

Stitch markers help. Place one every 20 stitches, for example, and count within each section. Adjust the expected count if the pattern includes shaping.

## Lifelines as prevention

A lifeline is a smooth contrasting thread passed through every live stitch of a checked row. It holds those loops if you need to rip back. It only protects stitches it actually passes through; it does not repair an earlier mistake. [Knit Picks explains the method](https://blog.knitpicks.com/how-to-use-a-lifeline-for-lace-knitting/).

Use a yarn needle to pass the thread through the loops while they remain on the knitting needle, including yarn overs that belong to that row. Avoid splitting the yarn and bypass removable stitch markers. Leave long, secured ends and record the row or chart position. Do not knit the lifeline together with your stitches on the next row.

Before a difficult section, choose a row whose stitches you can identify. If you rip back to it, return every held loop to a needle and verify the count and orientation before removing the lifeline. Resume with the row after the one recorded. For stitch structures you cannot identify, use a pattern-specific tutorial or get help.

## When a stitch runs to the cast-on edge

First look for an intact loop at the base of the column. If the cast-on is still intact, secure that loop and rebuild the rows above it in order.

If the cast-on stitch itself has come undone, the ordinary ladder repair has no intact base. Do not pull an arbitrary bottom strand through itself and assume the edge is restored. The reconstruction depends on the cast-on method: [VeryPink's cast-on repair](https://verypink.com/2019/02/06/recovering-a-dropped-cast-on-stitch/) is specifically for the long-tail cast-on. Secure the neighboring loops and follow a tutorial for your cast-on, or restart the affected work if you cannot recover the edge. Check the edge structure before continuing; blocking cannot replace a missing stitch.

## Fixing a twisted stitch ten rows later

An already knitted twisted stitch has crossed legs in the fabric. Changing only the mount of the current live loop will not undo a twist several rows below.

For one ordinary column without shaping, secure the intact stitch immediately below the error with a locking marker. Work to the column, secure its neighbors, then release only that column down to the held stitch, undoing the faulty stitch too. Rebuild each strand with the knit/purl sequence required by the fabric. Check that the crossing is gone, then check the final mount, count and tension. A wrong knit/purl stitch can be corrected this way too, but it is not a missing stitch.

If the column crosses a cable, increase or decrease, this simple sequence is insufficient. Use instructions for that structure or rip back to a verified row. Whether to correct a purely cosmetic twist is your choice; an unsecured live loop still needs to be secured.

## Mohair and other clingy yarns

If fibers cling together, as they can in brushed mohair or alpaca, separate each strand gently rather than pulling the ladder tight. Try a hook that fits through the loop and catches the whole yarn without splitting it; a slightly smaller hook can help with a tight loop.

Do not force stuck strands. For a nearby error, unknitting one stitch at a time is another option: put the freed loop from the row below onto a needle before undoing the next stitch. A distant or tangled error needs a recovery point you can identify.

## When to frog instead

Not every dropped stitch can be repaired with the same one-column method. Cables, lace, increases, decreases and colorwork require the actual stitch and yarn sequence at each level. Edge stitches and several adjacent dropped stitches also need their own reconstruction; secure each loose loop first.

If you cannot identify every strand and stitch, rip back to a checked lifeline or a row whose live stitches you can recover. The number of rows alone does not decide whether a repair is possible. Check count, pattern alignment, tension and the affected area's dimensions afterward; neither an invisible repair nor unchanged fit is guaranteed.

If you have knitted further rows while the stitch was missing, the upper gaps may not contain enough yarn to rebuild that column. Do not force short strands into new stitches; rip back to where the full-width ladder is available, or use a suitable repair with help.

## FAQ

**Do I need a specific crochet hook size?**
Close to your needle size or slightly smaller. Too large won't fit through the stitch. Too small and you can't grab the strand. Exact match not required.

**Will the fixed stitch look different?**
Sometimes. Gently ease slack through neighboring stitches without yanking. [Blocking](/articles/how-to-block-knitting/) according to the yarn's care instructions may help the fabric settle, but it does not fix crossed legs, missing strands or an incorrect stitch count, and it does not guarantee an invisible repair.

**Can I use a knitting needle instead?**
Yes, for a knit stitch that has unravelled one row: with the knit face toward you, put the loop on the left needle with its right leg in front and the ladder strand behind. Insert the right needle as if to knit, draw that existing strand through instead of using the working yarn, and let the old loop off. Return the new loop to its proper position and check the mount. The illustrated Nimble Needles tutorial linked above also shows the needle method; a hook is easier for several rows.

**What if I twist the stitch putting it back?**
A loop with its left leg in front is not necessarily a twisted stitch. [Patty Lyons explains how mount and needle entry work together](https://pattylyons.com/2016/03/tuesday-tip-how-avoid-twisted-stitches/). For the Western method here, support the loop with a needle or hook and remount it with the right leg in front before working through the front loop. Do not let it drop freely. A crossing already knitted into the fabric needs the separate repair above.

**Can I leave a dropped stitch unfixed and fix it after binding off?**
Do not leave the live loop loose. Secure it immediately, even in a finished piece. Simply covering the gap does not secure that loop. Rebuild the missing column where the structure allows, then secure its final loop using a finishing repair appropriate to the edge, or undo enough of the bind-off to incorporate it and bind off again. If you cannot identify the structure, get help before removing the marker.

**How do I prevent dropped stitches in the first place?**
Slow down at needle changes and check the working stitch before putting the project down. Wood or bamboo may offer more grip with slippery yarn. Fit point protectors on exposed needle tips during a break; the knob at the back of a straight needle does not stop stitches slipping off its working tip.
