US 8 (5.0 mm) is a useful first choice for a flat practice piece in smooth, light-colored worsted yarn whose label recommends this size. Bamboo or wood is worth trying if you want some grip. This is a starting recommendation for learning knit and purl stitches, not a size that suits every beginner project.

That size works for a reason. And there are situations where something different makes more sense. If you're also picking yarn, the [beginner yarn guide](/articles/best-yarn-for-beginners/) pairs with this one.

## Why US 7–9 is the sweet spot

US 7–9 (4.5–5.5 mm) is the [Craft Yarn Council's guideline range](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/yarn-weight-system) for Medium yarn, which includes worsted. The category describes yarn thickness, not the weight of a ball. It is a starting range, not a promise of gauge or a mandatory pairing.

For a first practice piece, medium needles and smooth, light, solid-colored yarn make the individual loops easy to inspect. A 5.0 mm needle is a practical middle choice within that range. Check the yarn label; if you already have a pattern, its yarn and gauge requirements guide the choice.

Smaller needles with suitable finer yarn are also valid starting tools. Bigger needles with thicker yarn are another option, but bigger does not automatically mean easier. Choose a combination you can hold comfortably and whose stitches you can see; needle size alone does not determine tightness, learning speed or how clearly the yarn shows stitches.

## A note on sizing systems

If you're shopping internationally or buying secondhand needles, you'll run into three different size systems. They don't agree.

US sizes use numbers, including very small sizes such as 000 and 00 and large sizes such as 50. The exact range varies by chart and manufacturer. Metric sizes are the actual diameter of the needle shaft in millimeters, which is what most of the world uses. UK sizes are the older system, mostly retired now, where higher numbers mean smaller needles (the opposite of US sizing).

The US 8 recommended here is 5.0 mm metric, which is what most European yarn labels will list. Old UK sizing would call this a 6. If a pattern lists only one system and you're buying needles in another, look for the metric equivalent on the packaging or check the [needle size chart](/tools/needle-size-chart/). Don't trust pure number conversion since US and UK numbers run in opposite directions.

These are nominal size labels, not an arithmetic conversion of US or UK numbers into millimeters. Manufacturer charts can differ, including fractional US sizes and sizes with no listed equivalent. Check the marked shaft diameter, behind the tapered tip; it is separate from needle length and tip shape.

## Needle type for beginners

Straight needles are a straightforward option for learning to knit back and forth. Two needles, one in each hand, with no cable or joining in the round to consider. Circular needles are an equally valid way to start.

A 10-inch straight needle is a useful length to try for a narrow practice piece. A 14-inch needle gives more room for stitches but also needs more space around your hands. Try the length with your own grip, and make sure the stitches fit without crowding or slipping off.

A 32-inch circular in US 8 also works for flat knitting: turn at the end of each row and do not join the ends. The length includes both tips and the cable. For ordinary knitting in the round, the assembled needle must be shorter than the work's circumference, with room to move the tips. It will not suit every hat or sock; a small circumference needs a shorter circular or another method such as magic loop.

## Material for beginners

Smooth bamboo or wood is a useful option to try if stitches keep sliding off. If stitches drag, compare a smoother surface, including metal, with the same yarn. Finish, tip shape, yarn and your grip all matter; the material name alone does not predict speed or dropped stitches.

Check that the surface and tip are smooth rather than rough or splintered. A very sharp tip is not required for learning knit and purl; if it keeps entering the yarn strands, try a rounder tip.

The [needle materials guide](/articles/knitting-needle-materials/) explains these differences in more detail. You can start with metal if it feels good to use. Check gauge again when changing needle material or model, even if the marked diameter stays the same.

## What to buy first

For a small practice piece: one pair of US 8 (5.0 mm) straight needles in bamboo or wood, around 10 inches long, or the circular option above. Start with a ball of smooth worsted yarn in a light, solid color, checking that its label recommends this size. Add scissors and a yarn needle for weaving in ends. For a complete scarf or dishcloth, check the pattern's yarn quantity and fiber requirements; one ball is not a universal project allowance.

That's it. Stitch markers, row counters, cable needles, needle gauges, everything else in the knitting aisle can wait until a specific project needs it.

You can add US 7 (4.5 mm) or US 9 (5.5 mm) if a swatch shows you need a different size. These are 0.5 mm steps around 5.0 mm, not a guarantee of any particular gauge change or coverage of most patterns. There is no need to buy all three before you start.

## What not to buy (yet)

A few common rookie purchases that mostly become drawer clutter.

A large interchangeable set can wait until you know which sizes, lengths and surfaces you like. Check its actual contents and compatible parts when you need it, rather than assuming it covers every future project.

Special shapes or extra-sharp tips are optional choices, not required beginner equipment. If standard needles hurt your hands, stop and reassess your grip, posture, session length, and tool choice. Persistent pain warrants advice from a doctor or occupational therapist.

Tiny needles are not a requirement for being a real knitter, but they are not inherently wrong for beginners either. Use them if your chosen yarn and project call for them and you enjoy handling the smaller stitches.

## When to branch out

Build your collection as your projects need it. You can follow a simple pattern from the start; there is no fixed number of practice projects to complete first. Check the needles you already own before buying another size.

An interchangeable circular set is worth considering once you know which sizes and lengths you use. Compare its contents and price with the individual needles you actually need.

The [needle size chart](/tools/needle-size-chart/) lists metric sizes and regional labels, with broad yarn-category guidelines. It helps identify an unfamiliar size; it does not select a needle that guarantees your pattern's gauge.

## Building tension

Uneven stitches are common while learning. Practise feeding the yarn consistently and forming stitches on the full-width shaft rather than only at the tapered tip. But practice and choosing needles for gauge are separate tasks: you do not have to wait until several projects are finished before adjusting the size.

For a pattern, make a swatch in its stated stitch pattern with the intended yarn and needles, using the same flat or circular method as the project. Make it larger than the measurement area, wash and dry it according to the yarn and project instructions, then count stitches and rows in the center over the distance the pattern specifies.

Too many stitches over that distance: try a larger diameter. Too few: try a smaller diameter. Swatch and measure again; a size step has no fixed effect. Check the fabric's feel and the pattern's row gauge as well. Matching stitch gauge alone does not guarantee suitable fabric or fit: finished measurements and ease still matter.

## FAQ

**Is it bad to start with metal needles?**
No. Start with metal if you can move and control the stitches comfortably. If they slip off, try a surface with more grip; if they stick, try a smoother one. Metal is not automatically harder for every beginner.

**Can I learn on circular needles instead of straights?**
Yes. Plenty of knitters learn on circulars and never own straights. Use the circular flat (back and forth, not joined) for your first project. The technique is identical.

**Do I need different needles for knitting and crochet?**
Yes. Completely different tools. A crochet hook has a hook and is used singly. Knitting needles have points and work in pairs or sets. Can't substitute one for the other.

**What if the pattern calls for a size I don't have?**
Treat the listed needle as a starting point and swatch with a nearby size you own if it suits the yarn. Choose the size that gives the required gauge and fabric. For a scarf or dishcloth, a different gauge may be acceptable if you are happy with the resulting dimensions and yarn requirement. Fitted garments need the pattern's gauge, finished measurements and ease checked.

**What about US 6 or US 10? Are they bad starting sizes?**
No. US 6 (4.0 mm) with suitable DK yarn and US 10 (6.0 mm) with suitable bulky yarn are reasonable alternatives. Check the label and swatch for the intended fabric. The smaller size does not by itself guarantee denser or more durable fabric across different yarns. US 7–9 with worsted is a useful starting range, not a ranking of every beginner's options.

**Should I buy in the same brand as my future set?**
Doesn't really matter for learning. The bamboo straights you start with won't connect to any interchangeable set anyway. Buy whatever's cheap, decent, and available. Brand loyalty can come later.
