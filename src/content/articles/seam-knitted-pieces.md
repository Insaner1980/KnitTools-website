A well-chosen seam joins the pieces securely and lets the garment hang as intended. Some joins blend into the fabric; others form a deliberate ridge. Choose the method to suit the edges, stitch pattern, and stretch the finished seam needs.

In stockinette, mattress stitch can create a nearly invisible seam by picking up horizontal bars one stitch in from the edge on each piece, drawing them together so the join looks like continuous fabric. The method you use depends on the seam direction: vertical edges (side seams), horizontal edges (shoulder seams), shaped pieces into openings (sleeves), or live stitches to live stitches (grafting a sock toe or shoulder).

Working in garter stitch or joining sleeves? Go straight to [mattress stitch for garter stitch](#mattress-stitch-for-garter-stitch) or [sleeve seams and setting in sleeves](#setting-in-sleeves).

## Seaming methods compared

| Method                   | Direction                  | Visibility             | Best for                        |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Mattress stitch          | Vertical row-to-row        | Nearly invisible       | Side seams, sleeve seams        |
| Horizontal mattress      | Bound-off edge to edge     | Looks like knit fabric | Bound-off shoulder seams        |
| Three-needle bind off    | Live stitch to live stitch | Visible ridge inside   | Stable shoulder seams           |
| Kitchener stitch         | Live stitch to live stitch | Nearly invisible       | Sock toes, grafted joins        |
| Whip stitch              | Depends on the edges       | Visible ridge          | Toys, linings, hidden seams     |
| Backstitch               | Depends on the edges       | Bulky inside           | Bag handles, load-bearing seams |
| Mattress stitch (sleeve) | Mixed row and stitch edges | Mostly hidden          | Set-in sleeves, armholes        |

</div>

The practical rule for sweater construction: mattress stitch for vertical seams, horizontal mattress or three-needle bind off for many shoulder seams, and Kitchener stitch when the pattern wants a grafted live-stitch join. Whip stitch, backstitch, and the rest fill specific niches.

## Mattress stitch (vertical seams)

Mattress stitch suits side seams and the long seams under sleeves. These instructions are for stockinette with ordinary knitted edge stitches; garter stitch uses different loops. With even tension, the join can look like continuous fabric.

Lay both pieces flat, right side up, edges touching and bottom corners aligned. Thread a blunt yarn needle with suitable seaming yarn. Leave a tail to weave in. Pass through each bottom corner from back to front to make a figure-eight connection, then gently draw the corners together.

At the bottom of the right piece, slide the needle upward under the horizontal bar between the first and second stitch columns. Do the same under the matching bar on the left piece. Return to the right at the previous exit point and pass under the next bar above it, then repeat on the left. Close the seam gently every few stitches and smooth it to its relaxed length. At the top, join the last corners, check the tension, and weave both tails securely into the wrong-side seam allowance.

**Seam allowance:** one edge stitch on each piece folds to the wrong side. The joined width therefore excludes two stitch columns in total; the stitches are enclosed, not decreased away. Follow the pattern's allowance rather than adding edge stitches to a finished piece.

For matching row counts and row gauge, one bar on each side keeps the rows aligned. Picking up two bars on each side is another established variant. If the counts differ over the same intended length, plan the distribution between matching points instead of insisting on a one-to-one rhythm.

### Mattress stitch for garter stitch

This joins **two side edges**, with the garter ridges running across the seam. One garter ridge spans two knitted rows. It is not a join between cast-on or bound-off edges. Use a blunt yarn needle and matching yarn; join the bottom corners as above before working the ridge pairs.

**1. Line up the pieces.** Lay them side by side with the faces you want on the outside facing up. Keep the cast-on edges at the bottom and align the ridges. A few locking stitch markers can help hold them in place.

**2. Find the edge loops.** Gently spread the edge to see the lower, U-shaped bumps and the upper, arch-shaped bumps. Pick up the outermost lower bump on the left piece and the outermost upper bump on the right. At the very edge, the lower bump often looks squashed beneath the more obvious upper one. Slide the needle upward **under the bump**, without splitting the yarn. Work right at the edge here, rather than a whole stitch in as you would for stockinette.

**3. Alternate sides.** Start at the bottom ridge, leaving a tail to weave in. Go under the lower bump on the left, then the upper bump of the matching ridge on the right. Move up to the next ridge and repeat. Keep picking up the same type of bump on each side, leaving the seaming yarn loose as you work.

**4. Close the seam gradually.** After a few stitches, gently pull the seaming yarn until the edges meet. Smooth out the fabric: the ridges should run across the join without puckering, and the seam should still stretch with the knitting. If the seam has shortened the edge, loosen the last few stitches and redistribute the yarn. Join the final corners and weave in the ends once the tension is even.

Slipped edge stitches can look different. Find the loops on a swatch before seaming the garment.

For close-up photos, see [Nimble Needles](https://nimble-needles.com/tutorials/seaming-garter-stitch-for-beginners/), or follow the demonstration at [Purl Soho](https://www.purlsoho.com/create/seaming-garter-stitch/).

### Reverse stockinette and textured fabrics

For reverse stockinette, keep the intended public purl faces upward. Sewing ordinary mattress stitch from the knit faces would put its seam allowance on the public side. For a purl-facing join, alternate under a lower U-shaped bump at the left edge and the corresponding upper arch at the right, then advance along the matching rows. Test the edge structure and tension on a swatch.

For cables and textured fabrics, inspect the actual edge stitches. A plain stockinette selvage can use the vertical method above; slipped, shaped, or patterned edges need the seam specified by the pattern or a tested adaptation. Keep motifs aligned rather than assuming every edge has the same bars.

### Half-stitch in for ribbing

Choose the seam from the rib sequence and the edge stitches. Where both edges end in a knit column and the pattern needs them to become one column, a half-stitch seam can work: pass under the outer leg of each edge knit V, alternately, advancing one row at a time. The remaining halves meet as one knit column. This is **within** a knit stitch, not the boundary between knit and purl columns. Other edge arrangements can use a full-stitch allowance. Swatch the join and check both the rib sequence and its stretch.

## Horizontal seams between finished edges

For two bound-off stockinette edges, lay the pieces right side up with the edges facing each other. Join the starting corners, leaving a tail. Work in the complete stitches just below the bind-off: on one piece pass under both legs of a V; on the opposite piece enter the center of a V and emerge through the next V's center, passing under the adjacent legs between them. Alternate, returning to each previous exit point and advancing one stitch. Match the sewn loops to the knitted stitch size, join the final corners, and secure the tails on the wrong side. The bound-off edges turn inside. Cast-on edges need their own alignment check; this sewn join does not require live stitches. [Purl Soho shows the stitch-to-stitch path](https://www.purlsoho.com/create/seaming-stockinette-stitch-stitches-to-stitches/).

## Three-needle bind off (horizontal seams)

Three-needle bind off joins and binds off two equal sets of live stitches together. It is often used for shoulders when the pattern calls for a supporting seam.

Hold the needles parallel, right sides together, with equal stitch counts and tips pointing the same way. Knit the first front and back stitches together onto a third needle, then knit the next pair together. Pass the older stitch on the third needle over the newer one. Continue knitting one pair and passing the older stitch over until one stitch remains. Cut the yarn with a tail, pull it through that last stitch, and weave it in.

The bind-off leaves a ridge inside and can support the shoulder. Its stretch depends on the yarn and tension; it does not make the shoulder incapable of stretching.

Both edges must have live stitches. If they are already bound off, carefully undo the bind-off and recover the original stitches on needles, or choose a sewn seam. Picking up new stitches along a finished edge is a different construction.

For a decorative exposed variation, work the three-needle bind off with wrong sides together. The standard ridge then sits on the right side instead of the wrong side. Some yoke patterns use this on purpose.

## Kitchener stitch (grafting live stitches)

Kitchener stitch joins live stockinette stitches with sewn loops that imitate another knitted row. It is often used for sock toes and other joins where the pattern calls for grafting without a seam allowance.

Hold the two needles parallel with wrong sides together, the same number of live stockinette stitches on each, and the tips pointing right. The stitches should be mounted without twists, with the right leg in front of each needle. Use a long tail from the first back stitch or a separate matching strand, leaving a tail to secure. Thread it through a blunt yarn needle. For setup, go through the first front stitch purl-wise and leave it on, then through the first back stitch knit-wise and leave it on. While at least two stitches remain on each needle, repeat this four-step rhythm: front needle knit-wise off, front needle purl-wise on, back needle purl-wise off, back needle knit-wise on. When one stitch remains on each needle, take the front stitch off knit-wise and the back stitch off purl-wise. Keep each grafting loop about the size of the neighboring stitches, then adjust the yarn along the graft before weaving in the tail.

The graft adds one stockinette row. Keep the written sequence beside you and adjust each sewn loop to match the surrounding stitches. The result depends on orientation and tension, not just completing the sequence.

Kitchener can also be used for grafting in the middle of a project, but cutting a finished garment is irreversible. Matching row gauge and stitch pattern is not enough: knitting direction matters, and top-to-top and top-to-bottom grafts can behave differently. Ribbing, cables, lace, and other vertically aligned motifs may show a half-stitch jog or another mismatch. Test the proposed graft in a swatch before cutting a finished garment.

## Whip stitch

Hold the pieces right sides together with matching edges aligned. Leave a tail and secure the starting corners. Pass the needle through both layers from back to front, then carry the yarn **over the edges** to the back. Repeat in the same direction at the next evenly spaced point along the seam. Keep the loops loose enough for the fabric, secure the final corner, and weave in the tails.

This version leaves a ridge inside. It can suit hidden seams, toys, or joined squares when its appearance and flexibility fit the project.

Try the seam on the actual fabric before using it where bulk or stretch matters.

## Backstitch (strong seams)

Align the pieces right sides together and mark the seam line. Leave a tail. Bring the needle through both layers to the front at B, one sewing-stitch length ahead of the start A. Insert at A, then emerge at C, one stitch length ahead of B. Insert back at B and emerge one stitch length beyond C. Repeat: go back to the previous stitch's end, then come up ahead. Finish by filling the last gap and secure the tails in the seam allowance. These distances describe sewn stitches, not knitted stitch counts.

Backstitch can make a firm supporting seam, for example where a pattern specifies it for a shoulder or bag handle. Strength and stretch depend on the fabric, yarn, stitch length, and tension. Test a sample instead of assuming it suits every load-bearing or stretchy edge.

## Setting in sleeves

### Seaming the length of a sleeve

For a sleeve knitted flat, the long seam from cuff to underarm closes it into a tube. You are joining two side edges. Use vertical mattress stitch along straight sections, or the garter-stitch version for a garter-stitch sleeve. Match the cuff edges and any stripes. This seam does not attach the sleeve to the body.

### Fitting a shaped sleeve cap into an armhole

Setting in a shaped sleeve cap is a different job. Along the curve, you will meet row edges, sloping sections, and bound-off stitches. You cannot follow one fixed row-to-row rhythm all the way around.

Match the pattern's sleeve-cap and armhole markers, including the underarm points. Align the cap center with the shoulder only when the construction calls for that placement. Ease in only the extra cap length intended by the pattern, distributing it between markers without folds. An unexplained size mismatch needs checking before sewing. Adapt the needle path to each section's edges, or use backstitch if specified.

Clips, locking markers, or suitable rust-proof pins help hold the matching points. They do not correct mismatched dimensions or guarantee a pucker-free seam. Check the relaxed fabric as you sew.

For garments knitted in flat pieces, you can often sew in the sleeve caps after joining the shoulders, before closing the sleeve and side seams. [Modern Daily Knitting explains how this differs from fitting a completed sleeve tube into an armhole](https://www.moderndailyknitting.com/community/how-to-set-in-a-sleeve/).

For a drop shoulder, a bound-off sleeve edge may meet the body's row edge. On the stitch edge, enter the center of one complete knit V and emerge through the next center; on the row edge, pass under the bar between the first and second columns. Alternate and return to the previous exit point on each piece. Distribute the rows against the stitches using their counts and gauges over the same seam length, checking between markers; there is no universal ratio. [Purl Soho illustrates this stitch-to-row seam](https://www.purlsoho.com/create/seaming-stockinette-stitch-stitches-to-rows/). Raglan seams follow their own shaped edges and assembly instructions.

## Seaming striped or colorwork pieces

Match intended color changes before sewing. A small row-count difference over the same intended length can sometimes be distributed by taking an extra bar on the side with more rows between markers. This does not repair differently knitted stripe sequences, and it is not guaranteed to be invisible. Stop and reassess if the fabric gathers.

For self-striping yarn where stripes are not meant to line up exactly, don't chase perfect matching at the expense of even tension. Self-striping seams that don't match still look better than puckered ones.

## Tips for cleaner seams

Use a blunt yarn needle. Sharp needles split yarn and poke through fabric instead of going between stitches. The eye needs to fit the yarn without shredding it, and the tip should slide between strands rather than pierce them.

Use a manageable yarn length that leaves tails for fastening. An excessively long strand can abrade as it passes through the fabric. If you need to join yarn, secure the old and new tails separately in the seam allowance without making a hard knot or lump.

[Prepare the pieces before seaming](/articles/how-to-block-knitting/) using the yarn or garment care instructions and a method tested on a swatch. Let them dry fully and compare their relaxed measurements with the pattern. Blocking does not guarantee the right size or remove stockinette's tendency to curl.

Match the ends and intermediate pattern markers before sewing, then check each section. Distribute only differences the construction can accommodate; do not stretch one piece to force a match.

Equal lengths do not necessarily contain equal row counts when the row gauges differ. Count the actual edges and distribute their counts across matching sections. A large or unexplained difference calls for checking gauge and knitting, not automatically taking extra bars.

Choose seaming yarn for strength, thickness, flexibility, colorfastness, and compatible care. Project yarn is often suitable, but bulky or fragile yarn may work better with a smoother, thinner alternative. Thin yarn is not automatically weak; test the finished seam for its intended use.

## Common mistakes

**A ridge shows on the public side.** Ordinary mattress stitch has a wrong-side seam allowance. Check which faces were upward, whether the needle path is appropriate, and whether the depth and tension stay consistent. For the stockinette method above, work between the first and second stitch columns, not “one bar in.”

**Wavy or puckered seam.** First loosen overtight stitches and spread the yarn evenly. Also check the matching points, row or stitch counts, and seam method: excess fabric or a wrong joining ratio can cause puckering too.

**Seam unravels.** Yarn tail wasn't secured. Weave the tail through the seam allowance in more than one direction before cutting.

**The pieces twist at the join.** Check the intended faces, top and bottom, and matching corners before sewing. Incorrect orientation or uneven tension can distort a join.

## FAQ

**What yarn for seaming?**
Use project yarn if it sews well, or a smooth alternative with suitable strength, flexibility, color, and care requirements. A thinner yarn can reduce bulk; verify it on a sample seam.

**How do I seam ribbing?**
Inspect the edge columns and the rib sequence. A half-stitch join passes under the outer leg of each edge knit V when two edge knit columns should become one. It is not worked between knit and purl columns, and some ribs need a full-stitch seam instead.

**Can I avoid seaming entirely?**
Yes. Choose patterns knit in one piece or in the round. Top-down sweaters, bottom-up circular yokes, and contiguous constructions can eliminate most or all seams; ends and other finishing still need attention. [Hats](/articles/how-to-knit-hat/) and [socks](/articles/how-to-knit-socks/) knit in the round have no side seams at all. If you hate seaming, filter pattern searches for "knit in one piece" or "in the round."

**My seam is puckering.**
First loosen overtight stitches and spread the yarn evenly. Also check the matching points, row or stitch counts, and seam method: excess fabric or a wrong joining ratio can cause puckering too.

**Should I weave in ends before or after seaming?**
Seaming tails are secured after the tension is settled. Weave each end separately into the wrong-side seam allowance, changing direction as appropriate for the yarn. Merely trapping a loose end in the seam does not reliably fasten it. Check security before trimming.

**Can I undo a seam without damaging the pieces?**
Identify the seaming yarn and unpick from an end, preserving the knitted strands. If cutting is necessary, isolate only the sewing strand before snipping it. Undo a three-needle bind off or a graft stitch by stitch and capture the released live stitches on needles. Fuzzy yarn can be difficult to separate without damage.
