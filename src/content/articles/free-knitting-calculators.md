How many stitches do you need for a 9-inch-wide scarf? How much yarn should you plan for a blanket? How do you space 8 increases across 64 stitches? Calculators can help with the arithmetic, but the result still needs checking against your swatch and pattern.

## Cast on calculator

Use gauge measured in the intended stitch pattern, with the project’s yarn, needles and finishing method. The [Cast On Calculator](/tools/cast-on-calculator/) uses stitches per 10 cm with a width in cm, or stitches per 4 inches with a width in inches. It divides gauge by 10 or 4 before multiplying by width, then rounds to the nearest even stitch count; an odd whole number rounds up. Switching units does not convert the entries. Choose the finished width or circumference, including any positive or negative ease, yourself. The calculator does not add ease, pattern multiples or edge stitches.

The formula is simple enough to do in your head for plain stockinette. It stops being simple when your stitch pattern repeats over 6 stitches plus 2 balancing stitches and you need a number that satisfies both the repeat and the width. The calculator gives you a starting count; you adjust for stitch repeats and edge stitches yourself.

A worked example in inches: gauge is 5.5 stitches per inch and target width is 9 inches. Select inches and enter **22** for gauge (5.5 × 4) and **9** for width. The raw count is 49.5 and the calculator returns **50**. For a 6-stitch cable repeat plus 4 edge stitches, 50 − 4 = 46 does not divide by 6. The nearest valid total is **52**: 8 repeats × 6 + 4. This is a manual adjustment. At a uniform 5.5 stitches per inch, 52 stitches would span about **9.45 inches**, not 9. A cable panel and stockinette edges may have different gauges, so measure the combined fabric before relying on that width.

Reach for the calculator any time you're adapting a pattern to your measurements, designing without a pattern, or [substituting yarn](/articles/how-to-substitute-yarn/) where your gauge differs from the pattern's. If the stitch pattern requires a multiple, use the calculator result as the starting point and then move to the nearest valid count. The [cast-on guide](/articles/how-many-stitches-to-cast-on/) covers that adjustment in more detail.

## Yarn estimator

Will three skeins be enough? The question haunts every yarn purchase, and guessing wrong means either running short mid-sleeve (with the dye lot discontinued) or sitting on expensive leftovers.

The result identifies nominal recommended supply, pattern-stated yardage or approximate required length. Supply is grams × label length ÷ label weight; it is not measured consumption. For yarns held together, each yarn is listed separately and the headline is their combined supply, not the length of one strand. Source ranges remain ranges. Conversion: 1 yard = 0.9144 m; round only the final display.

[Yarn Estimator](/tools/yarn-estimator/) — Reference quantities from named patterns; not a personalized consumption estimate. 133 reference cases from 19 patterns in 28 project categories. Wraps and pillow covers have no verified reference here. No scaling by yarn weight, gauge or dimensions; no automatic reserve.

For yarn substitution, calculate balls by hand from the required total length and the actual length per ball, using the same units and rounding up. If you need 660 yards and both yarns come in 100 g balls, 220 yards per ball means 3 balls; 164 yards per ball means 660 ÷ 164 ≈ 4.02, so **5 balls**. This assumes the same required length; matching the weight category alone does not establish a suitable substitute. Plan swatching, finishing and any extra allowance for the particular project before rounding up. One extra skein is not a universal margin; check dye-lot availability when buying.

## Convert gauge by hand

Your swatch gives you 22 stitches over 4 inches but the pattern specifies gauge per 10 cm. Or you measured over 2 inches and need to scale up. Simple division in theory. Easy to mess up when you're converting between metric and imperial and scaling from a small swatch at the same time.

To calculate stitches per 10 cm by hand, first convert any inch measurement to centimeters by multiplying by 2.54: 4 inches is 10.16 cm. Then divide the stitch count by the measured width in centimeters and multiply by 10. In this example, 22 ÷ 10.16 × 10 gives about 21.7 stitches per 10 cm. There is no separate interactive gauge converter here. The [gauge measurement guide](/articles/how-to-measure-knitting-gauge/) covers how to get a reliable swatch reading before you convert anything.

## Space increases and decreases evenly by hand

This is a manual method, not an interactive increase/decrease calculator.

Pattern says "increase 12 stitches evenly across the next row." 80 stitches on the needle. Where do the increases go?

80 ÷ 12 ≈ 6.67 is an average, not a complete instruction. For a flat row, one balanced plan is to make one twisted running-thread increase (M1) after original stitches **3, 10, 17, 23, 30, 37, 43, 50, 57, 63, 70 and 77**, then knit the final 3 stitches. Count only the original stitches when locating these gaps. Each M1 adds one without consuming an original stitch: 80 + 12 = **92**. All 12 increases lie between stitches, with 3 plain stitches at either end.

For 18 increases over 142 stitches on an established round, use sixteen 8-stitch groups and two 7-stitch groups, spacing the shorter groups around the round. Knit the last stitch of each group into its front and back (KFB): that stitch is included in the group count. The groups consume 16 × 8 + 2 × 7 = 142 original stitches and produce **160**. The [increase/decrease guide](/articles/increase-decrease-evenly/) explains these methods and remainder handling. Even spacing can suit a ribbing transition; it must not replace specified raglan lines, sleeve shaping or yoke placement.

A decrease consumes existing stitches. For example, repeat “knit 4, knit 2 together; knit 5, knit 2 together; knit 5, knit 2 together” four times: 4 × (6 + 7 + 7) = 80 original stitches, 12 single decreases, **68** remaining. This example reserves no edge stitches. If your pattern requires untouched edges, subtract them before distributing changes and work them separately; recalculate the intervals.

## Resizing a pattern to fit you

Calculators can help with a starting count, but they do not grade a garment. First distinguish body measurements from finished measurements. Suppose **38 inches and 44 inches are finished body circumferences**, already including the intended ease, and gauge is 20 stitches per 4 inches. For an unshaped tube, select inches and enter gauge 20, width 44: the calculator returns **220**, compared with 190 at 38 inches. A flat front or back needs its own width, not the entire circumference, plus any required seam or border adjustment. Armholes, neckline, sleeve caps and yoke shaping still require the pattern’s construction and row gauge.

This is where the math chains together. Cast-on for body width, increase spacing for yoke shaping, gauge conversion if you're working from a metric pattern in inches or vice versa. None of it is hard individually. Doing it all in your head while keeping track of where you are in the pattern is where mistakes creep in, especially on the third evening of a knitalong when you're tired.

## What separates a useful calculator from a bad one

A useful calculator makes its input units and rounding rule clear. Pattern repeats, edge stitches and fit still need checking; an even result alone does not satisfy every stitch pattern.

It should also be readable and usable on a phone. Check that you can see the selected units, inputs and result together, so that a correct calculation is not mistaken for a complete pattern adjustment.

## FAQ

**Are online knitting calculators accurate?**
The arithmetic can be exact, but the useful answer depends on the inputs and the rounding rule. One calculator rounds to the nearest whole number, another to the nearest even number, a third may round to the nearest multiple of 4 for a 2x2 rib. Check what your pattern requires and use a calculator that matches those constraints.

**Can I use knitting calculators without knowing my gauge?**
The cast-on calculator and manual gauge conversion need a measurement from your swatch. Choose a project, a named pattern size and a unit. The result is that pattern’s reference quantity, not a prediction for your own project.

**Why not just do the math by hand?**
You can. The calculator does the gauge-and-width calculation and rounds the result to the nearest even number. For a pattern with a 7-stitch repeat, adjust the result manually so the pattern stitches form complete repeats, with any edge stitches accounted for separately.

**How accurate is a yarn estimate when I haven't swatched yet?**
Reference quantities from named patterns; not a personalized consumption estimate. Use the linked original pattern for its exact size, yarn, construction and gauge. The quantity cannot predict a different fabric or altered dimensions. Check each color separately and the pattern’s swatching and reserve instructions before buying whole balls. Availability of the named yarns and colors is not guaranteed.
