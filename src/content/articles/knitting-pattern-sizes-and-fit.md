A pattern labeled "Medium" might fit like a tent on one person and feel tight on another. Size names alone cannot tell you how it will fit. First establish whether the pattern's numbers describe the intended wearer's body or the finished garment.

## Body measurements vs finished measurements

Your body measurement is what the tape says when wrapped around your chest, waist, or hips. The pattern's finished measurement is the dimension of the completed garment at that same point.

These measurements can differ or be equal, depending on the intended fit and the fabric. Compare the same part of the body and garment, in the same units.

Ease is the finished garment measurement minus the corresponding body measurement. For a chest circumference, a positive result means extra room, zero means equal circumferences, and a negative result means the garment is smaller before it stretches on the body. Ease alone does not determine fit at the shoulders, arms, or neckline.

## What ease means

Calculate ease from the finished, relaxed garment measurement in the condition specified by the pattern, usually after washing and blocking. Do not measure it stretched on the wearer.

**Negative ease** means the finished garment is smaller than the corresponding body measurement. It requires fabric that can stretch sufficiently and recover comfortably; ribbed hats and fitted socks are familiar examples. Stretch is a property of the knitted fabric, not a guarantee attached to a yarn label.

**Zero ease** means the finished measurement equals the body measurement. It can be an intentional close fit; it is not inherently a sizing error.

**Positive ease** means the garment is larger than the corresponding body measurement. The amount depends on the design and your preferred fit. More ease at the chest does not automatically mean more room at the upper arm.

The pattern's intended ease is a design decision. A cropped modern pullover might have 2 inches. A cozy cardigan might have 6. Neither is wrong. Different silhouettes.

## How to take measurements

You need a flexible tape measure (the sewing kind, not hardware store metal).

Chest or bust: wrap the tape around the fullest part, under the arms, level. Don't pull tight. Waist: the narrowest point, usually just above the navel (not at your jeans waistband, which sits lower on most people). Hips: widest point of the lower hip.

For sleeve length, follow the pattern's measurement endpoints. Center-back-neck to wrist crosses the shoulder and arm; underarm to wrist starts at the armpit, with the arm slightly bent. These are different measurements. Upper-arm circumference is measured around the widest part above the elbow.

Cross-back or shoulder width runs across the upper back from shoulder to shoulder. Follow the designer's diagram: a sloping shoulder-seam length is a different measurement. A suitable chest circumference does not guarantee that the shoulders fit.

Write these down. You'll reference them every time you pick a pattern size. A measuring buddy helps a lot for the back and shoulder measurements. They're hard to take accurately on yourself.

## Choosing your size

Read the pattern's sizing notes before choosing a size. A chart labeled "to fit chest" gives body measurements: use your body measurement and the designer's size-selection instructions. A chart labeled "finished chest" gives garment measurements: compare those with your body plus the intended ease. If both are shown, keep them separate. [Tin Can Knits explains this distinction](https://blog.tincanknits.com/2022/04/08/what-is-ease/).

For finished measurements, add positive ease to your body circumference, subtract the amount of negative ease, or use your body circumference for zero ease. The finished measurement already includes the garment's ease: do not add it again to that number.

Hypothetical example: your chest is 38 inches and your chosen target is 3 inches of positive ease, so the target finished chest is 38 + 3 = 41 inches. If the available finished circumferences are 38, 40, 42, and 44 inches, the two closest options give 40 − 38 = 2 inches and 42 − 38 = 4 inches of ease. Compare both with the design's intended fit and other dimensions before choosing. Neither is a universal comfort recommendation.

Don't choose "Medium" just because you buy that size in stores. A hypothetical Medium with a 44-inch finished chest gives 6 inches of ease on a 38-inch body; a 40-inch finished chest gives 2 inches. S/M/L, age labels, and regional clothing sizes must be read through that pattern's own chart.

## Reading a schematic

Many patterns include a schematic: a flat line drawing of the finished pieces with labeled measurements. This is the most useful part of the pattern for fit decisions.

The schematic shows widths and lengths at labeled points: chest, waist, hip, sleeve, upper arm, shoulders, and body length. Check whether each arrow means a flat width, a circumference, or a length. A flat width is not a circumference. For a simple symmetrical closed body, 20 inches across laid flat corresponds to approximately 40 inches around; separate panels and overlapping cardigan fronts need their own interpretation.

Find your size in the designer's stated order and mark its numbers throughout the text, tables, and schematic. Parentheses are one convention, not a universal layout. Read ahead for simultaneous shaping: a second instruction may begin before the first is complete.

Compare corresponding measurements, allowing for the intended ease. The chest might fit but the sleeves might be two inches short. The body might be longer than you want. The schematic helps you identify which dimensions need adjustment.

Check armhole depth against a well-fitting garment of similar construction, using the same endpoints. A shallow armhole can restrict movement, while a deeper one may be part of a deliberate dropped-shoulder silhouette. Chest circumference alone cannot settle this choice.

## When you're between sizes

Happens often. Your chest measurement puts you in one size, your hips need another, the arm length is wrong for either.

Choose a starting size by comparing the finished schematic and measurements with the intended ease and the garment's construction. Focus on the dimensions that control the hardest-to-alter parts of the design. In many fitted set-in-sleeve garments, shoulder and upper-torso fit may be a better starting point than the largest circumference. Blend between sizes or adjust other areas where the construction permits.

Length can be straightforward to change in an unshaped section worked to a measured length. Want 17 inches instead of 15? The difference is 2 inches. Before adding them, check waist and sleeve shaping, pattern repeats, pocket placement, and yarn needs. Upper-arm changes also affect the sleeve and its connection to the body.

If the body or sleeves are too long, identify where the surplus length lies. Shortening an unshaped section below the armhole may leave the shoulder construction unchanged, but keeping the original armhole depth is useful only if that depth already suits you. Height alone does not determine these proportions.

If you need extra length, assess body and sleeves separately. Extra rows may change the spacing of shaping or the end of a vertical repeat. Do not assume every instruction that says "work to length" can be extended without checking the remaining pattern.

## Choosing different sizes for body and sleeves

You may be able to combine body and sleeve sizes, but even adjacent sizes are not automatically compatible. For a set-in sleeve, check the sleeve cap against the armhole; their shaping and seam lengths must work together. For a raglan or circular yoke, check sleeve and body stitch counts, underarm stitches, shaping rates, and yoke depth.

Use the designer's adjustment instructions when available. Otherwise plan the connecting parts before knitting them: selecting an M sleeve and an S body is not itself a complete alteration. A pattern with separate sleeve or bust options can make this easier.

## Ease varies by garment type

Not all garments intend the same fit.

The [Craft Yarn Council's chest/bust ease guide](https://www.craftyarncouncil.com/standards/body-sizing) describes classic fit as about 2–4 inches of positive ease, loose fit as about 4–6 inches, and oversized fit as about 6 inches or more. These are descriptive guidelines, not guarantees for every fabric or body. Its body-size charts give body measurements, not a particular designer's finished garment dimensions.

Follow the pattern's intended ease and check the sample's measurements if available. If its description and your calculated ease seem inconsistent, check which size and measurement are being compared before deciding there is an error. Hats and socks need their own design-specific sizing guidance.

## How construction style affects fit

A sweater with a 40-inch finished chest can fit differently depending on its construction.

**Drop shoulder:** The shoulder line extends beyond the natural shoulder. The body is often relatively straight and roomy, but the sleeve width and armhole depth still affect movement.

**Set-in sleeve:** A shaped sleeve cap fits into a shaped armhole. Check shoulder width, upper-arm circumference, and armhole depth together; this construction can be used for more than one silhouette.

**Raglan:** Shaping lines run between the underarm and neckline. Body and sleeve dimensions interact through that shaping, so a matching chest circumference does not guarantee a suitable sleeve width or yoke depth.

**Circular yoke:** The upper body and sleeves join in a yoke with shaping distributed around it. Row or round gauge affects its depth. A saddle shoulder is a different construction, with a strip across the top of the shoulder; it is not another name for a circular yoke. See [Knit Picks' construction overview](https://blog.knitpicks.com/anatomy-of-a-sweater/).

Compare the schematic with finished photos and, where given, the model's measurements and worn ease. A photo helps explain the silhouette but cannot replace the measurement information.

## Short-row bust shaping

Many sweater patterns are written from standard chest and length measurements, not from a full-bust adjustment. If your front needs more length than your back, the front of a sweater can ride up while the back stays where it should. Short-row bust shaping adds extra length only at the front bust, without adding stitches.

Short rows turn before the end of a row, adding fabric locally. Bust darts may use progressively shorter or longer rows, depending on the method and direction of knitting. Follow a complete shaping method, including turn placement and how the turns are resolved; "turn before the previous turn" is not a universal recipe.

Some patterns include this option explicitly. Many don't. Adding it requires knowing your bust point's location relative to the underarm and roughly how much extra front length you need.

Extra front length does not add chest circumference. If you also need more width, that requires a separate adjustment. [Knitatude's alteration guide](https://www.knitatude.ca/blog/2019/8/8/how-to-modify-patterns-part-3-tips-and-tricks-to-manipulate-a-pattern) illustrates the role of short-row bust darts; the placement and amount must suit the chosen garment.

## The ease trap with different yarns

Fabric thickness, drape, stretch, and recovery can change how the same measured ease feels. A thin sweater and a thick sweater with 2 inches of ease need not feel alike, but yarn weight alone does not establish which will fit comfortably.

Do not automatically choose a larger pattern size when using thicker yarn. If the new gauge has fewer stitches per inch, the same stitch count already makes a wider piece. Check a washed, fully dry swatch, the intended fabric, and all affected dimensions before choosing a size.

## Practical sizing tips

If finished measurements are missing, ask the designer which measurements the sizing refers to. For a simple section, width in inches = stitch count ÷ stitches per inch. Hypothetical example: 200 body stitches around at 5 stitches per inch give 200 ÷ 5 = 40 inches in circumference. Use the count at the point being measured, not automatically the cast-on count. Ribbing, shaping, seams, and different stitch patterns can change the calculation.

Before choosing a size, [check your gauge](/articles/how-to-measure-knitting-gauge/) in the pattern stitch and working method, after the specified finishing and full drying. Changing needle size requires another swatch. A different pattern size or a stitch-count ratio does not automatically preserve row-dependent shaping, necklines, or sleeve caps. [Tin Can Knits' gauge example](https://blog.tincanknits.com/2016/04/07/how-to-knit-a-garment-at-a-different-gauge/) calculates horizontal and vertical effects separately.

To use a favorite sweater as a reference, lay it flat without stretching, in its usual clean, dry condition. For a simple closed body with equal front and back widths, measure across the chest and double it. This gives a finished circumference with its existing ease already included. Compare similar constructions and fabrics; account separately for openings, overlaps, and unequal panels.

If you're [substituting yarn](/articles/how-to-substitute-yarn/), assess the actual swatch's drape and recovery rather than judging by fiber name alone. Washing and blocking can alter dimensions, but they are not a reliable way to rescue the wrong size. Use the yarn's care instructions and the pattern's finishing method.

If you need one size for your upper body and another for your lower, blend between sizes. Follow the upper size for the yoke and chest, then increase or decrease toward the lower size at the waist where the construction permits. This isn't pattern grading; grading is the designer's process of developing the pattern across its size range. Some patterns include short-row bust shaping or separate upper/lower sizing. When the pattern doesn't, this modification takes some knitting math.

## FAQ

**Do I measure over clothes or directly?**
Follow the pattern's body-measurement instructions. Measure over the undergarments you intend to wear, or a close-fitting thin layer if specified. Measure without compressing the body. If you want room for layers, account for that in the intended ease rather than unknowingly counting the same allowance twice.

**My measurements span three pattern sizes. What now?**
Use the finished schematic and intended ease to identify which size best fits the design's hardest-to-alter area. In many fitted set-in-sleeve garments, that may mean prioritizing shoulder and upper-torso fit rather than the largest circumference. Blend between sizes or modify other dimensions only where the pattern construction permits.

**Does row gauge matter for fit?**
Row gauge may matter less than stitch gauge when a simple piece is worked to a measured length and has little or no row-count-dependent shaping. It matters whenever a fixed row count controls vertical dimensions or timing, including yokes, raglan shaping, armholes, sleeve caps, waist or sleeve shaping, and vertical pattern repeats.

**What if Ravelry projects say the pattern runs small or large?**
Treat project notes as clues. Compare the knitter's gauge, chosen size, yarn, finished measurements, and alterations, and check the designer's errata. Repeated comments justify checking the pattern; they do not by themselves tell you to choose a larger size.
